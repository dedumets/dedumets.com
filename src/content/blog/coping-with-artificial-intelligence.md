AI is taking the fun out of software development, and it's taking me with it.

## Expect the expected

It's not like it wasn't obvious this wasn't going to happen eventually, and it's not like this is the only industry being affected. Arguably, it's one of the few industries where AI actually makes sense, as opposed to the irony that is AI generated art, music, video games, and anything else rooted in creativity and expression. Similarly to how assembly got replaced with low-level languages, which got replaced with high-level languages, now English is replacing these high-level languages. But it being an obvious evolution to an old paradigm doesn't make it any easier for me to stomach.

Even since the launch of ChatGPT in 2022, LLM-based AI was able to provide a whole new world of troubleshooting and rubber-ducking for software development — it was just clunky to use. GitHub Copilot fixed that inconvenience with IDE integrations, and integrating their AI into code review flows on pull requests was incredibly useful when working alone. Claude Code gave the convenience of working however you want — I could finally fully integrate AI into my terminal workflow — supercharged with incredible models and direct access to your project. The speed of advancement in these tools has been very intriguing to watch, but, again, that doesn't make it any to stomach.

## Brain drain

It's not like having the actual knowledge of years of development has become entirely useless. Yes, modern models can probably make any project you want fully from scratch these days, assuming you have the budget for it, and it can also fix major issues in existing spaghetti codebases that a human would not dare to try and untangle, but being able to provide context of higher-level relationships or specific code fragments to look into can help drastically speed up the proccess. I can see why senior developers and product owners would love this stuff — if you spend a lot of your time writing your requirements to delegate to other people for the actual implementation, cutting out that middle man and doing all the work yourself with near instant feedback is a game changer. For developers lower on the foodchain, you can basically unlock your entire codebase as a new area of expertise. That's what happened to me when I had to start sifting through native Android code in React Native projects — I had no past experience with Java or Android APIs, but that no longer mattered, cause the LLM had all the experience in the world! But a vast majority of my admiration and what drew me into software engineering was specifically the aspect of learning these new technologies, troubleshooting these problematic areas of code, finding that one broken line of code.

Letting an AI write your code just shifts the responsibility that you as a developer have in the context of the change being made — you no longer write the code, but you have to review it. How's your code reviewing skills? How's your code reviewing skills when looking at fragments of a codebase you have no familiarity with? How's your code reviewing skills when dealing with tooling and APIs you have no familiarity with? I would guess that most people — at least most people I've worked with, and I'm including myself in that group — are pretty bad at reviewing code. I don't see why Linus Torvalds shouldn't use AI, despite many people trying to witchhunt him for it, but should _you_ — person reading this right now — be using AI? Or do we just collectively cross our fingers and hope the LLM will be fine and won't introduce new bugs or fix something that has been left intentionally wrong due to now being part of business logic?

I don't recall hearing anyone really mention this before, but one aspect that I am truly and incredibly struggling with since using more AI, is responsibility and knowledge of the project I'm working on. Yes, I should be aware of what, how, and when I built since I still prompted for it, and I should be aware of what the code does and how it's structured due to performing a code review. But a few minutes of prompting and a few minutes of reviewing is completely incomparable to slaving away at a problem for days on end, so that final result doesn't stick around as prominently, and sometimes doesn't stick at all. So, the project I put in a lot of time and effort into, which I knew like the back of my hand, is slowly morphing into something that _kind of_ looks like the thing I used to work on, but it's just not quite right — kind of like early AI image generation, the codebase suddenly has 6 fingers. This, then, leads me to become disinterested with the project, which causes me to become a liability, and requires me to actually put in _more_ effort to stay accountable with the direction the project is going. But is it always worthwhile to put in the effort for it? Recently, when looking at a few pull requests, I've found that the pull request itself is clearly AI generated. Well, okay, that's a workflow now. But, you keep looking further, and you see that the code review is also clearly AI generated. Then, you see the response to the code review, and guess what? AI generated. Has any human seen _any_ code in this entire PR?

## What about the money?

The hardware costs caused by the AI boom are totally absurd, but this is the one negative aspect of AI that I can excuse. Yes, I absolutely hate what the price of GPUs, RAM, and SSDs is now — it's ruining plenty of other hobbies of mine for me —, but I don't work for chipmakers. For all I know, it really is just supply and demand. These companies are currently the _only_ ones making money on AI, and opening new factories just to serve their demand can cause them to throw it all away if it's really a bubble. Similar story with data centers — yes, I'd prefer not to destroy forests or poison entire neighborhoods with noise pollution to get them built, but they're too expensive to build if there wasn't actually a need for them. The money needed for the actual AI companies to stay running, is mind-boggling, though.

As of writing, 1.5 trillion dollars have been spent on AI, resulting in 204 billion dollars in revenue[^1]. It's clear there is a ton of money to be made, but it still seems uncertain if there is any _profit_ to be made, unless you're the one providing hardware to these companies. The fact that current token spend is being massively subsidized is not much of a secret, but are the subsidies small enough that there actually is a way to bump up the product cost into profit while not losing the entire userbase? Only the AI companies know if it's possible, but ignoring this fact and putting your entire company at the mercy of AI seems downright ignorant. My point being, maybe granting the customer support team full beans on Claude to let them vibe code their own CRM tool may not be the best way to spend your token budget, because, at some point, someone _will_ have to actually think about what has been built and how to continue any work on it.

Even if AI remains reasonably priced, at some point, the economy is still going to start breaking apart. Specifically relating to software development, just look at what is going on in the reverse engineering space lately. PlayStation 5 emulation was born 3 months ago[^2], and already is playing games at full framerate, full quality. Console-exclusive games spanning over 20 years of game development are getting recompiled into native PC ports in the span of literal days. Adobe and Microsoft suites getting open source alternatives based on the original projects. Now, to be clear, I am absolutely pro emulation and reverse engineering, and I also think that Sony, Adobe, and Microsoft do plenty of scummy business practices to deserve a slap in the face every now and again, but it's a bit worrying when there is no effort required to just recreate something that is currently available on the market, instead of dealing with abandonware. AI bros were quick to laugh at artists getting mad about their art getting stolen to feed into AI models, but will we see the same reaction when their slop apps face the same fate? What motivation will these sectors have to continue innovating? What will happen with other industries when this money no longer exists, or when everything is always put on a paywalled and streamed rental? Jobs will be lost, disposable income will continue dropping, so less and less stuff will be bought, but at least your corporation sees a bigger green number next quarter, even if you're going bankrupt in 3 years due to all the jobs you've forced people to lose, right?

## Coping

The future is extremely scary thanks to AI regardless if it ends up succeeding or failing, but I can't control any of that. I can only work on myself. So, how can I keep myself sane and grounded, while also enjoying my work moving forward?

Well, for starters, I'm not giving up on AI until I have to. Don't get me wrong, if I could flick a switch that would return the world to a state where AI never existed, I'd do it in a heartbeat, but... I like having a job, having a place to live, buying things... Software development is going all-in on AI, and, at this point, pretending it doesn't exist or is unusable would be downright ignorant. But one thing I don't intend on succumbing to, is the fully agentic workflow. Acknowledging my ignorance in a project caused by overreliance on AI, I simply cannot afford to not look at my own codebase for guiding AI. I need to write my own pull request, so I'm aware of what changes I'm making and how they impact the project. And I need to work on my code review skills with the AI, since they'd be useful even without AI.

On the flip-side, spending less time thinking and writing code at work has given me new ideas and inspiration for personal projects, along with the energy to actually commit time to to them, which is pretty exciting. For these, I'm making a commitment to boycotting most AI functionality, so I can still keep my brain engaged and get back to the enjoyable parts of development — writing the code. I still may use some AI features for learning or rubber-ducking, though — it's a great tool for that, cause you can get super specific about your own issues and ideas, instead of risking getting stuck in tutorial hell when you don't know how to turn something theoretical you learned into a practical end result.

For these personal projects, I'm starting a new chapter in my life to get away from all the mess I've made in the past, both with and without AI. This site is actually one such case — this used to be a portfolio and a development blog. But I never finished anything to add to the portfolio, and I didn't write any blog posts, cause there was nothing to comment about in the portfolio. This new site is about **me**. I'm not trying to show off or hide anything anymore — if I feel like publicly commenting on something, I'll do it. Short-form content would go on [Bluesky](https://bsky.app/profile/dedumets.com), with long-form content staying here (also available as [RSS](/rss.xml)!), and I won't bother restricting myself to development topics. I also feel a bit more comfortable open-sourcing my projects, which, again, this blog is a prime example of — if you like something, you can take it, or if you see a typo, you can fix it. As I said — I'm not trying to change the world with with my code, but if it appeals or can help you — have at it.

Lastly, and I think more importantly to me than ever, is finding more analog hobbies to replace software development. After finally putting out all my thoughts into this post, I've seen a resurgance of inspiration to play guitar once again. I've also started cooking and running a lot lately, and both of these have, honestly, been way more enjoyable activities than they used to be whenever I did them before. Still can't get myself to read books consistently, though... maybe with some more time.
